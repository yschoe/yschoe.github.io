Background and purpose

This project asks how a very small organism can let recent events remain causally active in its present behavior. A Paramecium-like cell has no nervous system, yet its membrane voltage, ion-channel state, intracellular calcium, second messengers, chemical adaptation, and energy all decay on different time scales. Together those overlapping traces form a compact “extended present.”

The swimmer does not read a mathematical food-gradient arrow and point toward it. Attraction instead emerges through klinokinesis: improving conditions promote fast, smooth forward swimming; worsening conditions make a voltage/Ca²⁺ avoidance event more likely. Random paths are therefore retained longer when they happen to improve the chemical situation.

Membrane and ion-channel abstraction

Resting voltage is near −25 mV. Attractant tone, cAMP, attractant onset, posterior touch, Ca²⁺ feedback, and correlated channel noise all contribute to a continuously changing voltage target. Anterior touch and attractant loss depolarize the model; food onset and posterior touch tend to hyperpolarize it.

Crossing about −17.5 mV opens an abstracted voltage-gated ciliary Ca²⁺ pathway. The resulting fast Ca²⁺ pulse reverses ciliary beating and makes the cell swim backward. As ciliary Ca²⁺ falls, the cell pivots, then resumes forward swimming. Channel availability falls after a Ca²⁺ event and recovers slowly, representing Ca-channel inactivation/refractoriness. Somatic Ca²⁺ supplies a slower memory and a compact biphasic surrogate for Ca-sensitive Ca²⁺ and K⁺ conductances; individual channel species are not fitted separately.

Chemical memory, cAMP, and energy

Current food affects a glutamate-like cAMP pathway. A separate slow adaptation variable remembers recent food concentration. Food below that memory produces an “off-response”; food rising faster than memory produces an onset signal. cAMP and adaptation therefore preserve chemical history after the immediate concentration has changed. Feeding replenishes energy, while basal metabolism and rapid movement consume it.

How to play

  1. Select Add food, then click or tap in the arena. The green chemical field spreads beyond the bright food core.
  2. Select Add obstacle and place barriers. A front collision usually evokes backward swimming and a pivot.
  3. Use Move selected to place the highlighted organism near a patch or barrier.
  4. Use Probe front, Touch front, or Touch rear to inject controlled mechanical events.
  5. Add multiple organisms, then use Select next to change which history appears in the plots.

Desktop shortcuts: Space pause, 1–4 speed, A/P front/rear touch, N add organism, C cycle the selected organism, G food field, T trails, E CSV, S arena image, R reset, and H or ? help. Right-click adds an obstacle, Shift-click relocates, and middle-click probes the front.

Body colors

  • Cyan: ordinary swimming.
  • Red: recent physical contact or a contact-evoked avoidance sequence.
  • Green: meaningful uptake very near the center of a food patch.
  • Amber: a non-contact avoidance or reorientation sequence.

How to read the scrolling plots

Every plot shows the most recent 42 simulated seconds. The right edge is “now.” Markers align events across all four charts: a green circle is a food encounter, a red diamond is contact, and an amber triangle is non-contact avoidance.

Scope and scientific caution

This is a transparent, hypothesis-generating educational model. It is not a quantitative fit to a particular species, does not resolve individual channel molecular kinetics or hydrodynamics, and is not evidence that Paramecium is conscious. Its parameters are deliberately compact and editable so measured channel kinetics, spatial Ca²⁺ compartments, and recorded trajectories can replace the simplified terms later.
